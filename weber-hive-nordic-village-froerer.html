FOLLOW THE DEED · CASE 05

The Trustee Signed His Own Deal

A Weber County commissioner's own entity transferred land for zero dollars, then dissolved. A PID trustee signed the same land into the district he oversees, for ten dollars, on behalf of his own employer. And the resort's real owner — a sitting French regional president co-leading his country's 2030 Olympic bid — never told his own government he owned it.

Start here

What is this? Three separate people, three separate roles, all touching the same 512 acres in Ogden Valley: a county commissioner whose own company transferred the land for nothing, then dissolved; a public infrastructure district trustee who signed that land into the district he sits on, for ten dollars, while working for the company on the other side of the deal; and the resort's actual owner, a French official who never disclosed the ownership to his own country's transparency authority.

What's the goal? Not to argue that Nordic Village shouldn't be developed. It's to test whether the public process that approved $80 million in bonding on this land actually had the disclosures, recusals, and transparency it was supposed to have — regardless of how good or bad the finished resort turns out to be.

The rubric

What benefits the public collectively, not any one actor's reputation or track record, and not a personal opinion about who's involved.

1. Power — Does it add or remove a check on power that the public, collectively, would otherwise have no say over?

2. Transparency — Can the public actually see the process: posted hearings, itemized financials, disclosed conflicts — regardless of who ends up holding the final decision?

3. Financial accountability — Who actually pays, who's protected from paying, and is that structure disclosed plainly or buried in a bundled figure?

4. Environmental impact — What's the effect on water, wetlands, and wildlife, and does the process treat that as a real constraint or a box to check?

5. Community impact — Who bears the cost or holds a historical/cultural stake in this decision, and did the process give them real standing to be heard, or was their interest treated as a formality?

The Froerer chain

Nordic Valley Land Associates LLC → Liens subordinated (1/24/23) → Land transferred, $0 (1/27/23)

Nordic Valley Land Associates LLC (NVLA) was managed by two entities — Kier Development LLC and J&G Investment Partnership LLC — both listing Gage Froerer as manager. J&G held trust deed liens on all four Nordic Valley parcels. Those liens were subordinated January 24, 2023, and three days later the property transferred to Nordic Village Venture LLC for zero consideration. Eleven days after that, Froerer personally filed J&G's dissolution. Kier Development and NVLA were both dissolved on the same later date — January 20, 2026 — after the PID bonds had already closed and after Froerer had already voted to authorize them.

No disclosure filed for four years, then a false one

Froerer was elected to the Weber County Commission in 2018 and, per the Standard-Examiner's own 2022 reporting, had "been involved in the trust that owns the land for 20 or so years" — meaning the NVLA relationship long predates his time in office. From 2018 through most of 2022, he filed no conflict-of-interest disclosure for this relationship at all. The Nordic Valley village proposal formally emerged in October 2021; he participated in deliberations on it without disclosing or recusing for roughly ten months after that. He recused from a vote for the first time on August 16, 2022 — but still filed no written disclosure. A written disclosure only came on November 20, 2022, and only after a Weber County resident, retired attorney James O'Brien, filed a formal complaint with the Utah State Auditor on October 24, 2022, alleging Froerer had failed to disclose a material conflict. That disclosure revealed a family trust's 16% stake in NVLA — four years into his term, and only once a citizen forced the question.

"We have spoken about this in public meetings in the Valley and never hide anything. He talked about it, you reported it several times, we spoke about it — so it is public knowledge and Gage Froerer recused himself." — Laurent Jouffray, email to the Standard-Examiner, December 19, 2022

That's Jouffray himself, on the record, four years before telling Shauna Miller in a public exchange that "Froerer was never a partner in the project" — a direct contradiction of his own earlier statement, not just a disputed claim. But Jouffray's "public knowledge" framing leaves out how that knowledge became public: not a disclosure filed at the start of his term, or when the proposal emerged, but only after a citizen's formal complaint to the State Auditor, four years into holding office alongside the relationship.

What actually changed is the timing: whatever standard the 2022 episode set — disclosure only under pressure, after the fact — wasn't met at all before the vote that mattered most. Froerer filed no conflict-of-interest disclosure before the November 20, 2024 PID creation vote — the same window as the zero-consideration land transfer and, per Shauna Miller's own property and corporate records, a handwritten note on his February 2023 disclosure form falsely claiming he'd divested from NVLA entirely. He hadn't. What isn't established, and remains an open question, is whether he holds any further undisclosed paperwork or partnership interest with Skyline Mountain Base/Sorep or with John Lewis beyond what's documented here — Froerer's relationship with the French-controlled side of this deal predates the false divestment claim by years, which makes it the more likely place to look, though it isn't confirmed.

The full arc, laid end to end: four years with no disclosure on file, a citizen complaint forces one, and the eventual claim that he'd fixed the underlying problem — divestment — doesn't hold up against the property and corporate records. Caught, then only partly corrected, and even that partial correction turns out not to be true.

The Lewis-Dunleavy chain

D&R Investment Properties (2021) → Liberty Village LLC (2022) → GWC Capital LLC (2024) → Nordic Village PID No. 1 (2026)

A separate parcel (220100071, now titled to the PID, valued at $2.637 million) ran through Liberty Village LLC — formed July 2022, dissolved June 2024, existing for exactly the window this parcel was assembled. Its members: Edenview Co. (Shane Dunleavy's entity) and Lewis Group Holdings LLC (John Lewis's own holding company) — the same development partnership already documented behind Eden Crossing and Wolf Creek Resort elsewhere in this project.

Who is Clyde?

GWC Capital and Clyde Capital Group are both divisions of Clyde Companies, Inc. — one of the largest, longest-running construction and development conglomerates in Utah. Founded in 1926 as W.W. Clyde & Co., a heavy civil construction firm, the family business grew into a parent company (formed 1997–98) now spanning eight subsidiaries — including Geneva Rock, Sunroc, Beehive Insurance, and the two capital/development arms named in this piece. Roughly 5,000 employees, an estimated $2.5 billion in annual revenue, still majority family-owned. The founder's brother, George Dewey Clyde, later served as Governor of Utah. This isn't a small local developer — it's one of the state's most established, politically connected construction families, operating a real estate development arm through the same entity structure documented here.

The cleanest conflict in the file

Brandon Henrie is simultaneously a Nordic Village PID trustee and President of Clyde's GWC Capital development division — not a lower-level manager, the head of the specific division doing this deal, per Clyde's own public statements. He personally signed the transfer of this parcel from GWC Capital into the PID he sits on as trustee — for $10. This isn't a trustee who happens to hold a financial stake in a company voting on a matter that benefits it. This is the same person acting as the seller's signatory and the buyer's trustee in one transaction.

A possible eligibility defect, not just a conflict

Utah Code §17B-1-302 requires PID trustees to reside within the district's boundaries. Weber County confirmed, in response to a records request, that Jouffray is registered to vote in Box Elder County, not Weber County. If that residency status holds, it raises a more basic question than any disclosure gap: whether Jouffray was ever legally eligible to serve as a Nordic Village PID trustee at all, separate from and in addition to the ownership-stake conflict already documented above.

Every one of these votes happened on a Weber County planning body, under the County Commission's authority — not the new Ogden Valley City's. Froerer and Warburton have a documented political relationship dating to 2018. When asked directly whether she'd disclosed her Utah Forward PAC role at her appointment or in any subsequent meeting, Warburton declined to answer and instead accused the reporter of being paid to ask the question — an on-record non-denial, not a defense. No ethics complaint or formal review of her conduct has been publicly reported as filed.

The water itself

The lawsuit isn't a dispute over paperwork — it's a fight over a genuinely scarce resource in a mountain valley with a documented history of running short. The Wolf Creek Water and Sewer Improvement District currently has access to just one culinary water source, the Warm Springs well, though a second is reportedly in progress. Back in 2021, the same district imposed a moratorium on new water-service "can and will serve" letters during drought conditions, halting new home construction on the hillside below Powder Mountain — this isn't a new problem the current lawsuit invented.

Dunleavy's own public position, on the record to KSL: the district has sufficient capacity but is "just actively choosing not to use it." The district's own statement says otherwise — one working source, a second not yet online. A second, separate developer (P.C. Luminary LLC, pursuing a 60-unit project called Powder Canyon at Wolf Creek) filed a nearly identical lawsuit against the same district the following month, echoing the same complaint. P.C. Luminary is genuinely unconnected to the Lewis/Dunleavy network — Utah corporate records show its own registered agent (Judson Tolman Pitts, Taylorsville) and manager (George Wright, Eagle Mountain), a different address and different principals entirely. Its actual parcel, per Weber County's own plat records, is 220160117 — 2.735+ acres in the "Village at Wolf Creek" development, inside the same Wolf Creek Water and Sewer Improvement District footprint. Two unrelated developers making the same complaint against the same district within a month of each other is a real pattern in the same water system Nordic Village's own resort development sits inside, not an isolated dispute between one developer and one district.

The bigger structural fact behind the scarcity: Pineview Reservoir sits directly in the middle of Ogden Valley — Nordic Village's own footprint — and it is the primary water source for roughly 120,000–135,000 downstream residents across Ogden, South Ogden, Riverdale, and other parts of Weber County. Ogden City drilled around fifty artesian wells in the valley itself over a century ago; when the reservoir was built in the 1930s, those wells were capped beneath the future lakebed and piped straight into the city's own system. That buried wellfield is the direct ancestor of the pipeline Ogden City is still rebuilding today. In 2026, Weber Basin Water Conservancy District declared a mandatory 20% allocation reduction due to drought. New development competing for water inside the valley isn't just facing general regional scarcity — it's competing against senior downstream obligations to a much larger city population that were built into this system nearly a century before Nordic Village existed.

The 100% claim

When Brook Cole signed the original PID petition — February 6, 2024, notarized November 14, 2023 — he represented himself as the sole 100% surface property owner. That claim wasn't just later superseded by a more complicated reality — it was already false when he signed it. The Nordic Village Master Development Agreement's own beneficiary identification document, which lays out the real split, is dated March 14, 2023 and October 13, 2023, both months before Cole ever notarized or filed the petition. The real owners' names were kept off the public record while the split that named them already existed on paper.

For roughly the entire petition process, the public saw one owner where there were, at minimum, three — and the largest single stake belonged to the French company whose real beneficial owner, per Mediapart's later reporting, never told his own government he held it.

The France side

Mediapart, a major French investigative outlet, published a full account of who actually owns the resort. Its reporting, independently confirmed and safe to cite directly:

Renaud Muselier declared to France's own public transparency authority (HATVP) only that he is an unpaid "manager" of the two US entities that hold Nordic Valley — never disclosing that he owns a ski resort. — Mediapart, Karl Laske

Muselier is president of France's Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur regional council and co-president of Solideo Alpes 2030, the body organizing infrastructure for France's 2030 Winter Olympics. He has personally led outreach between France's Olympic organizers and Utah's 2034 Olympics organizers — including a January 2024 delegation trip where he met Gov. Cox — while holding this undisclosed stake. Nordic Valley (then called Wolf Mountain) was bought in 2014 for 3–4 million euros out of a bank following a post-subprime bankruptcy; Sorep, the French company behind the purchase, injected 10.6 million euros into the US holding entities in 2023. A French politician has since publicly called for Muselier's resignation.

Laurent Jouffray — the same PID trustee already documented above — is described by Mediapart as a French former chartered accountant who represents Sorep in the US. His own Utah disclosure separately admits Skyline Mountain Base holds a 50% stake in Nordic Village Venture LLC, the developer entity he votes to fund as trustee.

The local half of the deal

Sorep didn't buy the resort alone. Utah trade press coverage from the time names Josh Richards, then a 37-year-old Ogden-area entrepreneur, as the local partner who formed the arrangement with Sorep specifically to finance the purchase — the French company came in as the financing partner to a locally-initiated deal, confirmed by Richards himself to the Standard-Examiner at the time. The purchase closed in January 2014, no price disclosed publicly at the time (Mediapart's later reporting puts it at 3–4 million euros). The resort's own stated pitch at acquisition was explicitly the opposite of what it's become: a "family friendly" resort at "a fair price," positioned deliberately against Powder Mountain and Snowbasin — Richards described those resorts as "Audis or BMWs" next to Wolf Mountain's "Subaru or minivan." The resort's original name, before a 2005 rebrand to Wolf Mountain, was Nordic Valley — the name the current project has since revived.

A SEPARATE, OLDER OLYMPIC CONNECTION Nordic Valley, under its old name Wolf Mountain, was used as a training venue by the Austrian ski team for the 2002 Salt Lake City Olympics — decades before Muselier's ownership and unrelated to the 2030/2034 conflict above. Worth keeping the two facts distinct.

Who actually pays

The $120,870,032 project is funded through a mix of developer money and public financing mechanisms — and roughly a third of it lands on the public, split between people inside the PID boundary and people who will never live there.

SourceAmountWho pays it
Developer equity/debt$79,942,771The developer, privately
PID debt$24,355,615Property owners inside the PID boundary, via a special levy on top of ordinary property tax
Tax increment (School, Water, Fire — 50% each, 15 yrs)$11,525,738Diverted from what those three districts would otherwise collect — not from PID residents specifically
Tax increment (Weber County — 75%, 15 yrs)$5,045,908Diverted from the county's own general fund growth
The Weber Fire District's own 30-year model shows exactly what this costs them: its annual revenue from this land, absent the diversion, would be $856,315/year once the district's participation ends in 2042. During the diversion period (2027–2041), the district is held to roughly half that, $428,158/year — the same $856,315 figure only kicks in the year the CRA participation window closes. That doubling, in the district's own published table, is the diversion. Multiply the same structure across the School District and Water District, each also giving up 50% of their own growth on this land for 15 years, and the same pattern repeats.

This is the indirect mechanism already documented elsewhere in this project: diverting future tax increment from the School District, Fire District, and Water District (50% each) and the County (75%) doesn't take money directly out of anyone's pocket — it takes away growth those entities would otherwise have collected, forcing them to either raise rates on everyone else in their district or cut services to compensate. People who never set foot in Nordic Village, inside the same school, fire, and water districts, absorb part of the cost of a development they have no ownership stake in and, per the record above, weren't even told the true ownership of until well after the county had already committed.

Against all five criteria, the Lewis/Dunleavy parcel transfer itself is the closest thing to routine in this file — ordinary land assembly, traceable through normal records. But the network around it isn't: the same partnership's lobbyist connection and litigation against a public water district still grade badly on financial accountability and community impact. Everything touching an actual PID trustee, county commissioner, or planning commissioner grades badly on power and transparency, for the same underlying reason each time: the person deciding and the person benefiting were the same person, without a disclosed recusal.
