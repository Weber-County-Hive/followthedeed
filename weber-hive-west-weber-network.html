FOLLOW THE DEED · CASE 03

More Lots, Same Playbook

Two developer networks, one UIPA project area, and the same move repeated across both: get a rezone or a density amendment, add units, and let a public label — "affordable," "attainable" — do the political work of getting it approved.

Start here

What is this? Two separate development networks operating inside and around the same West Weber corridor — one built around Pat Burns/Lync and Black Pine's Bennet LLC assembly, the other around Nilson Homes' JDC Ranch — both using the same basic move: get more units approved on the same acreage, market part of it as affordable, and let elected officials champion the label on the record.

What's the goal? Not to argue that density itself is bad, or that "affordable housing" language is dishonest by definition. It's to test a specific claim against the record: does adding units to get a project approved actually lower prices for buyers, or does it mostly mean more total units sold at prices close to market rate?

The rubric

This piece touches both kinds of decisions this project tracks — legislative/regulatory authority and public financing structures — so it's graded against all five questions, not a subset. Each one is scored Good, Neutral, or Bad for the public — not for any one actor's reputation or track record, and not a personal opinion about who's involved.

1. Power — Does it add or remove a check on power that the public, collectively, would otherwise have no say over?

2. Transparency — Can the public actually see the process: posted hearings, itemized financials, disclosed conflicts — regardless of who ends up holding the final decision?

3. Financial accountability — Who actually pays, who's protected from paying, and is that structure disclosed plainly or buried in a bundled figure?

4. Environmental impact — What's the effect on water, wetlands, and wildlife, and does the process treat that as a real constraint or a box to check?

5. Community impact — Who bears the cost or holds a historical/cultural stake in this decision — tribal or otherwise — and did the process give them real standing to be heard, or was their interest treated as a formality?

Below, each topic in this piece gets its own grade across all five, rather than one score for the whole thing — they don't all land the same way, and flattening them into a single verdict would hide that.

Network one: Black Pine, Pat Burns, and the Gibson land

Gibson Farms (family land) → Bennet LLC / Black Pine → 550-ac "Bennet Farms" TN/FB rezone

Weber County's own May 2025 hearing notice confirms the entire 550-acre Bennet Farms assembly is "formerly Gibson Farms" — and it's not a standard rezone into an existing density tier. Filed as ZMA2024-16 / ZTA2024-07, the application asks the county to create a new zone district, "Traditional Neighborhood" (TN), dual-zoned with the county's existing "Form-Based" (FB) designation — a category built for this project rather than an existing one the land already qualified for. The land's current zoning is a patchwork (A-1, A-2, R1-15, R-3, per county staff), not uniformly agricultural. One real mitigating detail: the proposal also includes an Open Space (O-1) zone specifically along the Weber River Corridor, and per local reporting the attached development agreement runs 30 years — longer than any other single agreement documented in this piece — with a "town-architect review process" written in to enforce design standards over that span.

The same developer, smaller projects, same move

Herrick Estates (Bitton Dee rezone) — agricultural (A-1) to R1-15, grew from 91 lots on 30.65 acres at the Planning Commission stage to 93 lots on 32.112 acres by the time it reached the County Commission — lots added mid-process, not reduced. Lot sizes in the final subdivision range from 6,000 sq ft up to roughly 25,000 sq ft, with density concentrated at the smaller end. West Creek Subdivision — same developer, same ag-to-R1-15 move. Sage Creek Subdivision (Plain City, 30 lots) — same pattern again, plus a road-cost-sharing agreement that puts the county on the hook for baseline repairs while the developer's own cost-share only kicks in at Phase Four.

At the November 18, 2025 Western Weber Planning Commission meeting approving the project's later phases (now filed as "Bitton Estates," a further name variant on the same development), a neighboring property owner, Alan Franke, raised a concern on the record: an irrigation ditch running through "the Herrick property" feeds his own land to the north, and despite raising this with Burns directly back in March and with the project's engineer more recently, the engineer wasn't even aware the ditch served properties farther north. A promised follow-up visit to walk the property hadn't happened by the time of this meeting. The Commission approved the phase 7–0 regardless, with staff noting the concern would be "reviewed at future steps."

The attached Development Agreement, "Longhorn Estates Subdivision" — between Weber County and Pat Burns/2020 LLC, signed March 26, 2024 by then-Commission Chair James H. "Jim" Harvey — caps the project at 116 lots over a 10-year term, requires a $7,500-per-lot park-district donation, and includes one real safeguard: if the developer defaults, the zoning automatically reverts to Agricultural (A-2). The Board of Trustees transitions from appointed to elected only as certificates of occupancy are issued to 60%, 75%, and 90% of units respectively — meaning the fully appointed, Lync-dominated board can remain in place for years before any seat becomes elected.

This is a pattern, not a one-off: PCC PID's own 2023 creation notice uses nearly identical language — "consent to the creation... and waiver of the protest period has been obtained from all property owners." The same formal legal mechanism, an explicit written waiver of the public's statutory objection window under the Local District Act, appears in two separate districts built by two separate developer networks in this file. Worth checking every other Weber County PID/IFD creation document against this exact clause going forward, rather than treating "no real hearing" as an informal pattern each time it's found.
Worth stating plainly: Sage Creek's structure is the honest version of what PID/CRA defenders often claim about their projects generally — "it doesn't cost the schools or the county anything." For Sage Creek, on these numbers, that claim is actually true. For PCC PID, on the same numbers, it isn't. Pat Burns is tied to all three structures. The point isn't that every financing tool in this network is extractive — it's that the same person is behind an honest one, an extractive one by association, and a third whose own governing board is, on paper, mostly his own construction company's staff.

Can these districts grow after creation?

Yes, and this isn't hypothetical for at least one of them. PCC PID's Governing Document has already been amended twice to expand the Districts' Annexation Area — most recently August 12, 2025, following the Districts' own board resolution, and once before that. Each expansion adds territory to the same $150M debt structure without a new petition or a new public hearing; the district's own board resolves to expand, and the county commission ratifies it.

Longhorn IFD's Governing Document has the same mechanism built in from the start: a section titled "Original District Boundaries; Designated Expansion Area," and a separate provision stating the Governing Document "may be amended by passage of a resolution of the Board approving such amendment" — no new petition, no new protest-period waiver, because that mechanism is only required once, at creation. The same board that's two-thirds Lync Construction controls whether and how the district grows from here.

Why this matters beyond the district's own boundary: this project has already documented the general mechanism elsewhere — when a PID or TIF-capturing district's footprint grows, the future tax-value growth on that added land gets captured by the district's own restricted structure instead of flowing to the county, school district, and fire district's general funds the normal way. Those entities still have to provide services — roads, sheriff patrol, fire response, schools — to the growing population inside the expanding boundary, while the added tax growth that would normally help fund those services is redirected instead. Whether PCC PID's own annexation-area expansions specifically overlap with the larger UIPA West Weber tax-differential capture zone documented elsewhere in this project has not been confirmed and isn't asserted here — but the underlying mechanism (board-controlled growth, no new public process, added service burden without matching added general-fund revenue) is the same pattern already established for TIF/PID structures generally, and it applies at the county level, not just within whichever boundary a given district's own materials describe.

Longhorn IFD directly connects to a finding already documented elsewhere in this record: two LMSA annexation resolutions (both dated 3/11/25) show Pat Burns/his "2020 LLC" petitioning to annex parcels named "Longhorn I" and "Longhorn II" into LMSA — with no public hearing required, since the petitioner and the property owner were the same person. The district's actual creation petition confirms this directly: parcels #10-036-0066 and #10-177-0017 are the exact "Longhorn I" parcels, and #10-035-0072 is the same "2020 LLC" Section 13 parcel already tracked elsewhere in this network — three previously separate threads converging into one district.

Note also: "Sage" appears in two unrelated places in this file and should not be conflated. Sage Government Solutions (Hartley brothers, tied to Sage Creek's IFD) is a separate entity from Sage Development Group — Stephanie Russell's own DBA, used for her LMSA lobbying work. Same word, two different operations, no confirmed connection between them beyond the name.

One more connection worth stating: Pat Burns/Lync Construction sits as a Builder Director on the board of the Northern Wasatch Home Builders Association — the same trade association Mike Schultz, chief sponsor of H.B. 491 elsewhere in this project's Great Salt Lake research, previously led. Burns was newly elected to that board for the 2025 term, per NWHBA's own announcement — the same general window as his LMSA board appointment (June 2024) and the rest of this network's activity. That's a direct tie between the two men through an industry board seat, separate from and in addition to anything documented above.

UIPA — the authority the whole corridor sits inside

UIPA's West Weber Project Area covers 8,967.74 acres, adopted May 20, 2024 over a 1,515-signature opposition petition. Daniel Stephens — Black Pine's own principal — personally attended and spoke at UIPA board meetings alongside Russell. The Plan itself projects 14,000 housing units as part of this "industrial" project area's eventual buildout, with data centers explicitly named as a targeted recruitment industry.

Indigenous heritage — what the record shows, and what it doesn't

This area was historically occupied by Chief Little Soldier's band (the "Weber Utes") down to the Great Salt Lake, part of the Northwestern Band of the Shoshone Nation. No documented claim of a burial ground on the West Weber footprint itself was found in any source checked for this piece — that specific allegation, raised separately, does not have primary-source support here and isn't asserted.

What is documented: the Plan's own Cultural Resources/Tribal Lands section consists of one sentence — "Northwestern Band of the Shoshone Nation is located in the vicinity of the project area" — compared to several pages on wetlands treatment in the same document. Tribal contact named: Chairman Dennis Alex. Separately, at UIPA's April 29, 2024 board meeting, Darren Parry (former Chairman, Northwestern Band of the Shoshone) opposed the project on resource-depletion and future-generations grounds — not a burial-ground claim. Brad Parry (current Vice Chairman) has spoken publicly about wetlands and site selection in this same area, also not raising a burial claim.

The honest read: whatever the environmental and cultural stakes of this land are, the Plan's own tribal consultation record is thin relative to its treatment of wetlands — one sentence against multiple pages — regardless of whether any specific burial-ground allegation holds up.

Network two: Nilson Homes, JDC Ranch, and the same move on paper

South Ogden-based Nilson Homes proposed a 725-unit development on 240 acres of former farmland at JDC Ranch, near Farr West. In 2024, county commissioners — Sharon Bolos specifically — approved a development agreement amendment adding 275 more units, branded "attainable," bringing the total to 1,000 units on the same acreage. Nilson's own VP, Bryan Bayles, told commissioners the plan was developed working with Steve Waldrip, Gov. Cox's senior housing adviser — the same official this project has separately documented threatening a county's funding over local housing opposition, and holding expanded statutory authority under H.B. 68.

The sharper finding: a standard, market-rate townhome in this same development already lists at $399,975 — complete, no stated exclusions. The "Attainable Starter Home" program's own base price cap is "no more than $400,000," before garage, basement, and landscaping are added back in. On these numbers, the specially-branded "attainable" product isn't meaningfully cheaper than an ordinary market-rate townhome already for sale in the same community — it may end up costing more once fully built out. The single-family "attainable" price is a real discount only against the single-family market-rate lines (Grove, Creekside, Orchards single-family, $529K–$625K), not against the townhome tier already on the market at essentially the same starting number. This isn't just an asterisk in Nilson's own marketing — a Weber County Planning Division staffer, Tammy Aydelotte, described the units on the record to county commissioners at the time of approval: "These are starter homes. They are around 900 square feet, no garages, either no basement or no finished basement in there." At the same 2024 meeting, the attainable units were quoted at $330,000–$400,000 against $500,000+ for the development's market-rate homes — and one real, positive detail worth including: the attainable units carry a 10-year deed restriction meant to keep them out of corporate/rental hands.

Nilson Homes is also the first builder in the state to use a new $300 million low-interest builder-loan program specifically created for "affordable" housing, with Gov. Cox personally appearing at a company event to promote it. Current marketing for other Nilson communities advertises payments "starting as low as $1,984/month" — based on a 3-2-1 interest-rate buydown, a temporary financing structure, not a reduced sale price.

The topics genuinely don't grade the same, and flattening them into one verdict for "this piece" would hide that. Against all five criteria above, Sage Creek's structure passes every one it's assessed on — no power transferred beyond its own stated purpose, no diversion, no hidden figures. PCC PID and the Bennet rezone fail the most criteria, on power and transparency both. The West Weber Plan's environmental grade lands as "mixed" for a specific, checkable reason: its own text makes wetlands-mitigation funding "recommended," while the wetland inventory and 600-ft buffer sit right next to it as "mandated" in the same document. Its community-impact grade is a separate, worse finding: a single-sentence tribal-heritage section against multiple pages on wetlands, in the same Plan.
