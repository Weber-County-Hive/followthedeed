FOLLOW THE DEED — CASE 02

The Backstop That Became a Bypass

A homebuilder used a family ranch, two landowner LLCs, and a 2024 state law to try to create its own town in Summit County. The bill that would have given the county a say failed in the Senate by a single vote.

The Weber County Hive Source: disclosures.utah.gov, le.utah.gov, Utah Division of Corporations, Park Record, KPCW
00 — THE BILL

H.B. 510, "Municipal Incorporation Modifications" (2026)

Sponsored by Rep. Tiara Auxier (R-Morgan/Rich/Summit) in the House and Sen. Kirk Cullimore (R-SD 19) in the Senate.

In plain EnglishImagine a law that lets someone build a whole new town without ever asking the county for permission. This bill would have made them at least talk to the county first — but the county still couldn't say no.

H.B. 510 would have given counties a formal role before a "preliminary municipality" petition — the tool Ivory Homes used at Lost Creek and Philo Development used at Wasatch Highlands — could move forward. It did not give counties a vote or veto over whether the town gets created; that decision stays with the Lieutenant Governor's office either way. What it added was friction: a required coordination period, the right to commission a traffic study, and the right to audit and dispute the feasibility numbers.

None of these four outcomes turned on a county's opinion of the project. A filing-order quota and a map line decided three of them. Nothing has decided the fourth yet — and nothing currently in Utah law, including every version of H.B. 510 discussed below, would have given a county the power to decide it either. If Ivory Homes had simply filed a few weeks earlier, before the statewide cap filled, nothing on the books would have stopped Lost Creek — it would likely be sitting exactly where Wasatch Highlands is now.

What "preliminary municipality" actually means under Utah law

A 2024 state law (S.B. 258) lets landowners — one, two, or three of them, no more — petition the Lieutenant Governor's office directly to form a new town, bypassing the county's own planning and zoning process entirely. The state, not the county, decides whether the new town is "feasible."

Full statutory history, the four 2026 Wasatch Back applicants, and the fight over reforming this law live in the companion Bill-Tracker case file — see below.

03 — THE VOTE

One more "yes" would have added a county check. It didn't come.

In plain EnglishThe bill needed 15 yeses to pass. It got 14. One more yes, from anyone, would have changed the outcome.

H.B. 510 (2026) would have added friction to that process — a coordination period, a traffic study right, an audit-and-dispute right — but never gave counties an actual vote over whether the town gets created. It passed the House 68–0. In the Senate, it failed 14–12, one vote short of the 15 needed.

Four sitting senators who personally took campaign checks from Ivory Homes voted against the bill. A fifth didn't vote at all. Ivory Homes' own Senate sponsor for the bill voted yes.

How this is graded

This isn't a personal opinion of the bill. The grade below asks two questions, applied the same way to every bill in this series — not whether any one person likes the outcome:

1. Power: does it add or remove a check on power that the public, collectively, would otherwise have no say over?

2. Transparency: does it add or remove what the public can actually see about the process — posted studies, public hearings, disclosure requirements — regardless of who ends up holding the final decision?

Design and outcome are graded separately because a bill can be structurally sound and still fail to become law, or vice versa; collapsing the two into one score would hide which one actually happened.

If it had passed — the bill's design
C+ PARTIAL — ADDS FRICTION, NOT A VOTE

Even in its strongest form, this bill never gave the county a vote or veto over whether the town gets created — that decision stays with the Lieutenant Governor's office either way. On power, that's the core failure. On transparency, it does better: even the weakened Senate substitute kept the requirement that a county post the feasibility study online, and the original bill would have added a public traffic study and a formal dispute record. Real transparency gains — but they don't translate into anyone actually holding decision-making power over the outcome.

04 — THE NEXT STEP THIS LAW MAKES EASY

The same small ownership that bypasses zoning also makes a tax district trivial to form

Two separate Utah laws share the same structural quirk — and it isn't a coincidence worth ignoring.

In plain EnglishIf you and two friends own an empty field, you can vote to turn it into its own town. Then you can vote again — just the three of you — to add a tax bill to it. Nobody else ever gets a vote, either time.
🏘️
Form a town
≤3 landowners can petition the state to incorporate a preliminary municipality (§10-2a-502(2)(d))
=
🧾
Tax that town
100% of surface owners must consent to form a PID (§17D-4-201) — trivial when it's the same ≤3 people

A preliminary municipality, by law, can only be formed by one, two, or three landowners (Utah Code §10-2a-502(2)(d)) — that's what lets a company like Ivory Homes or Philo Development create one without going through a county at all.

Separately, forming a Public Infrastructure District (PID) — a body that can levy a special property tax on homeowners and issue bonds against it — requires a petition signed by 100% of surface property owners within the proposed district (Utah Code §17D-4-201).

In an existing neighborhood, getting 100% of owners to agree to a new tax on themselves is a high bar. On land that hasn't been sold to anyone yet — where the only owners are the same one, two, or three people who just formed the town — "100% consent" is nothing more than those same people signing twice. The preliminary-municipality law's small-ownership requirement and the PID law's unanimous-consent requirement fit together by construction: whoever controls enough land to form the town controls enough land to tax it, before a single resident ever gets a vote.

This is a structural observation about how two Utah laws interact, not a claim that Ivory Homes or Philo Development has filed, or intends to file, a PID on this land. Neither Lost Creek nor Wasatch Highlands has a confirmed PID on record as of this writing. The mechanism is available; whether it gets used here hasn't been shown.

The pattern is already live elsewhere in the same city: Heber City approved three PIDs on Sept. 1, 2026 for a separate, unrelated project — D.R. Horton's "The Highlands" — financing $22 million of a $40 million infrastructure cost through 31 years of special assessments on homeowners, in exchange for a non-binding promise to cut $40,000 off each home's advertised price.

05 — THE PID QUESTION: WHO ACTUALLY PAYS

Even a "straight" PID doesn't make the public whole

In plain EnglishA PID makes new homeowners pay extra property tax for their own streets and pipes. That sounds fair on its own. But it usually only covers what's inside the development's fence line — the county still has to pay for the road connecting it to everything else, and there's rarely enough left over to grow fire and police coverage to match.

There are two separate mechanisms people mean when they say "PID," and they matter differently here:

🏠
A "straight" PID assessment
An extra property tax, capped at 15 mills, paid only by homeowners inside the district. Funds bonds for infrastructure inside the district's own boundary. Doesn't redirect money away from the county, school, or fire district.
≠
↩️
Tax increment financing (TIF)
Freezes a base year, then redirects the growth in tax revenue above that baseline into a bond fund — instead of letting the county, school district, or fire district collect it. A real diversion. Not every PID has this; only ones structured with a tax-increment component do.

Here's the part that survives even if no TIF is ever attached: a straight PID typically pays only for infrastructure inside its own boundary. It does not typically cover the county road that connects the development to everywhere else, regional water-source development, or the added strain on countywide services like fire and sheriff coverage — those still land on whoever was already paying for them, unless every applicable impact fee gets fully assessed and actually collected.

Be blunt about what a TIF diversion actually does: when a county, school district, or fire district gets that growth revenue taken away, there are exactly two ways to close the gap — raise the tax rate on everyone who isn't inside the district, or cut the services that revenue would have paid for. There is no third option. In practice, both happen.

This isn't hypothetical. In Weber County's Nordic Village PID, an interlocal agreement pledges up to $30 million in future growth revenue from the school district and $6 million from the fire district into the PID's own bond fund. Neither entity gets that money back. They either serve the same growing population with less funding than they'd otherwise have, or they raise rates on every other taxpayer in the district — including people who live nowhere near the development — to make up the difference.

Neither project has a confirmed TIF-backed PID on record as of this writing (see Section 04).

Wasatch County Manager Dustin Grabau said exactly this, on the record, about the Wasatch Highlands proposal specifically:

"That road has never been planned and does not have adequate right of way to accommodate a significant increase in traffic. If that's not included in the analysis, and they go ahead and proceed to build without making regional transportation improvements, that puts a burden on all the county residents to the benefit of three property owners." Dustin Grabau, Wasatch County Manager — Park Record, Clara Hatcher, 2/20/2026

Grabau also said the county has no adequate water/sewer infrastructure for this density and is under no legal obligation to build it, since the project sits outside the county's own projected growth area: "Honestly, I don't know that we would have the ability even if we wanted to." He separately noted that if impact fees like the Wasatch County Fire District's aren't assessed on the new development, "that may put a proportionate burden on the rest of the county through other tax sources to make up the difference of the increased service level."

The throughline: whether or not a PID or TIF ever formally attaches to Lost Creek or Wasatch Highlands, the same shape keeps showing up. New development creates real infrastructure and service costs. A PID assessment can cover some of it — the part inside the fence line. Everything outside that line, and everything a missed impact fee should have covered, gets paid by people who were already there, whether or not any tax-increment mechanism is ever used.
